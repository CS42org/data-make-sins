# 50 Common Data and Statistics Mistakes

Inspired by How to Lie with Statistics, The Art of Thinking Clearly, and Thinking Fast and Slow.

## Sampling: who got counted

1. **Biased sample.** The people you measured are not like the people you talk about. In 1936 Literary Digest polled over 2 million people from phone books and car registration lists, predicted Landon would win, and Roosevelt won by a landslide.
2. **Voluntary response.** Only people with strong feelings bother to answer, so results look extreme. Online news polls and one star reviews work this way.
3. **Survivorship bias.** You only see the winners because the losers left no data. In WWII engineers wanted to armor the bullet holes on returning planes. Abraham Wald said armor the clean spots, because planes hit there never came back.
4. **Small samples (law of small numbers).** Small groups give extreme results by pure chance. In the US, the counties with the lowest kidney cancer rates are mostly small rural ones. So are the counties with the highest rates.
5. **Selection effect.** The group was already special before you measured it. Harvard graduates earn a lot, but Harvard picks people who would earn a lot anyway.
6. **Cohort effect.** A 1991 study said left handed people die 9 years earlier. Wrong: older generations were forced to write with the right hand, so there were simply fewer old left handers to count.

## Averages and percentages

7. **The wrong average.** A few huge values pull the mean up. If Elon Musk walks into a cafe, the average customer is a billionaire. Ask for the median.
8. **Averages hide the spread.** A river can be one meter deep on average and still drown you in the middle.
9. **Percent of a tiny number.** "Cases doubled" can mean from 1 to 2. Always ask: doubled from what?
10. **Percent vs percentage points.** A tax rising from 10% to 15% is a 5 point rise but a 50% increase. Media pick whichever sounds bigger.
11. **Relative risk without absolute risk.** In 2015 WHO said processed meat raises bowel cancer risk by 18%. In real numbers that is roughly from 6 in 100 to 7 in 100 people over a lifetime.
12. **Doubling a tiny risk.** In 1995 the UK warned that a newer pill "doubled" blood clot risk. It went from 1 in 7,000 to 2 in 7,000. Thousands of women stopped the pill and abortions rose sharply.
13. **Counts instead of rates.** "The US has the most COVID cases" mostly means the US has a lot of people. Compare per 100,000.
14. **Cherry picked base year.** In 2021 many companies reported "sales up 200%", compared to the locked down spring of 2020.
15. **False precision and numbers with no source.** "10,000 steps a day" came from a Japanese pedometer brand name in 1965, not from research. Decimals and round numbers do not make a guess accurate.

## Charts

16. **Cutting the vertical axis.** In 2012 Fox News showed a tax rate going from 35% to 39.6% with the axis starting at 34%, so the second bar looked five times taller.
17. **Cumulative charts.** Adding each period to the last makes the line always go up. Apple showed cumulative iPhone sales in 2013 to hide slowing growth.
18. **Scrambled axis order.** In May 2020 Georgia's health department sorted dates out of order so COVID cases appeared to fall.
19. **Pie charts that add up to 193%.** Fox News, 2009: Palin 70%, Huckabee 63%, Romney 60%, all in one pie.
20. **Pictures that grow in two dimensions.** Doubling the height of a money bag icon makes it look four times bigger.
21. **Choosing the time window.** "No warming since 1998" only worked because 1998 was an unusually hot El Niño year. Start a year earlier or later and the trend is clear.

## Cause and effect

22. **Correlation is not causation.** Countries that eat more chocolate win more Nobel prizes (published in a top medical journal in 2012). Both track national wealth.
23. **Spurious correlations.** With enough data something always matches. Nicolas Cage films per year track swimming pool drownings closely.
24. **Reverse causation.** "Napping is linked to dementia." Or early dementia makes people nap. The headline picks one direction.
25. **Post hoc (after this, so because of this).** New York credited its 1990s crime drop to tough policing. Crime fell just as much in cities that did nothing similar.
26. **Regression to the mean.** Extreme results drift back to normal on their own. Israeli flight instructors thought shouting worked because a bad landing is usually followed by a better one anyway.
27. **Simpson's paradox.** In 1973 Berkeley admitted 44% of men and 35% of women. Inside each department women did as well or better. They had applied to the hardest departments.
28. **Ecological fallacy.** True for groups, false for people. Richer US states vote Democrat, but richer individuals tend to vote Republican.
29. **Missing control group.** "80% of patients improved after treatment." How many improved with no treatment? Most colds end anyway.

## Probability and mind traps

30. **Base rate neglect.** A disease hits 1 in 1,000. A test is 99% accurate. You test positive. Your real chance of having it is about 9%, not 99%. Most doctors get this wrong too.
31. **Prosecutor's fallacy.** In 1999 a UK court jailed Sally Clark after an expert said two cot deaths in one family were a 1 in 73 million chance. He ignored how rare double murder is, and that the two deaths were not independent.
32. **Gambler's fallacy.** At Monte Carlo in 1913 black came up 26 times in a row. Players lost millions betting on red because it was "due".
33. **Clustering illusion.** Random dots always form clumps. Londoners in WWII believed German bombs targeted certain districts. The pattern was chance.
34. **Availability bias.** Vivid events feel common. After 9/11 many Americans drove instead of flying, and around 1,500 extra people died on the roads the next year.
35. **Framing.** Patients chose surgery more often when told "90% survive" than when told "10% die". Same fact.
36. **Denominator neglect.** "1,286 out of 10,000 die" was rated scarier than "24.14 out of 100 die", although the second is almost double.
37. **Anchoring.** People who saw a rigged wheel land on 65 guessed more African countries are in the UN than people who saw 10. "Was $199, now $99" works the same way.
38. **Ignoring the margin of error.** A poll at 48% vs 46% is a tie, not a lead. Forecasts gave Clinton 70% to 99% in 2016, and people read that as certain.
39. **Confirmation bias.** We notice the numbers that agree with us. The same jobs report gets "unemployment falls" and "wages stall" headlines on the same day.

## Research and media tricks

40. **Loaded questions.** In 2013, 46% of Americans opposed "Obamacare" but only 37% opposed "the Affordable Care Act". Same law.
41. **The semi attached figure.** Prove one thing, claim another. "More doctors smoke Camels than any other cigarette" was a real 1940s ad and said nothing about health.
42. **Advertising surveys.** Colgate's "80% of dentists recommend" was banned in the UK in 2007. Dentists could name several brands, and most also named competitors.
43. **P hacking / Texas sharpshooter.** Test many things, report the one that "worked". Cornell's Brian Wansink told a student to keep slicing the data until something looked good. More than a dozen of his papers were retracted.
44. **Multiple comparisons.** Scan enough data and noise looks real. In 2009 researchers put a dead salmon in an fMRI scanner and found "brain activity".
45. **Publication bias.** Positive results get published, negative ones sit in a drawer. Of 74 antidepressant trials, the published papers made 94% look positive. The real figure was 51%.
46. **Significant is not important.** With huge samples, tiny effects become "significant". Facebook's 2014 mood study on 689,000 users found an effect too small to notice.
47. **Extrapolation.** Assuming today's trend runs forever. A 2004 Nature paper extended sprint records and concluded women will outrun men in the 100m by 2156.
48. **Big data overfitting.** Google Flu Trends matched search terms to flu, then in 2013 predicted roughly double the real cases and was later shut down.
49. **Goodhart's law.** When a number becomes the target, people game it. Wells Fargo set account targets, and staff opened millions of fake accounts.
50. **Lead time bias.** "US prostate cancer survival 82% vs UK 44%" (Giuliani, 2007). The US screens earlier, so patients count as "surviving" longer even when they die at the same age.
