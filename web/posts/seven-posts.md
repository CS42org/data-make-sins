# Seven ways numbers fool you

Seven short posts. One mistake each. Real cases. Each post has a matching animated figure in the web page (six-traps.html) and a list of the real news sources. Screenshots of the articles are not included for copyright reasons. Open the links and take your own.

---

## Post 1 of 7. The planes that came back

**Mistake: survivorship bias**

In 1943, US planes came back from the war with bullet holes. The holes were on the wings and the tail. The army wanted to put armour there.

Abraham Wald, a statistician, said no. These planes came home. The planes hit in the engine never came back. So the engine needs the armour.

**The same trap today.** The bestseller Good to Great studied 11 winning companies chosen from 1,435. Within seven years one was bankrupt and one was rescued by the government. The book never looked at the companies that did the same things and failed.

**Better method.** Before you count, ask who is missing. Count the planes that left, not only the planes that returned.

**Ask:** Who is missing from this data? The data you can see is the data that survived.

**From the news**
- Freakonomics (New York Times), 2008: "From Good to Great … to Below Average". Steven Levitt checked the 11 winning companies in the bestseller. Circuit City went bankrupt, Fannie Mae was rescued by the government, and the 11 together did worse than the stock market. https://freakonomics.com/?p=2878
- Background on Abraham Wald and the bombers: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivorship_bias

## Post 2 of 7. The average that nobody earns

**Mistake: mean instead of median**

Add all the salaries and divide by the number of people. That is the mean. One billionaire in the list makes the mean very big.

The median is the person in the middle. Half earn more, half earn less. A billionaire moves the middle by one person only.

**The same trap in every wealth report.** The average American adult owns about 550,000 dollars. The person in the middle owns about 110,000. Both numbers are true. Only one describes a real person.

**Better method.** When some values are very big, use the median. Show the range from the 25th to the 75th person too, so readers see the spread.

**Ask:** Is this the mean or the median? If the word is only average, ask which one.

**From the news**
- UBS Global Wealth Report, 15 Aug 2023: wealth per adult in the US, mean 551,000 dollars, median 108,000 dollars. Both numbers sit side by side in the report. Most stories quote only the first. https://www.ubs.com/global/en/media/display-page-ndp/en-20230815-global-wealth-report-2023.html

## Post 3 of 7. Bacon and the 18 percent

**Mistake: relative risk without absolute risk**

In 2015 the World Health Organization said processed meat raises bowel cancer risk by 18 percent. Newspapers said bacon causes cancer. Sales fell.

Out of 100 people, about 6 get bowel cancer in their life. With bacon every day, it is about 7. One extra person. That is the 18 percent.

**The same trap in 1995.** UK health officials said a new contraceptive pill doubled the risk of blood clots. The risk went from 1 in 7,000 to 2 in 7,000. Many women stopped the pill. Abortions rose by thousands the next year.

**Better method.** Always show the real numbers next to the percent: 6 in 100 becomes 7 in 100. Also say how many people must eat it for one extra case. Here it is about 100.

**Ask:** 18 percent of what? A big percent of a small number is still a small number.

**From the news**
- The Guardian, 26 Oct 2015: "Processed meats rank alongside smoking as cancer causes, says WHO". https://www.theguardian.com/society/2015/oct/26/bacon-ham-sausages-processed-meats-cancer-risk-smoking-says-who
- Cancer Research UK, 26 Oct 2015: the same finding in real numbers. Out of 1,000 people who eat little processed meat, about 56 get bowel cancer. Out of 1,000 who eat the most, about 66. https://uicc.org/news/how-interpret-iarc-findings-red-and-processed-meat-cancer-risk-factors

## Post 4 of 7. The axis that starts at 34

**Mistake: a chart that does not start at zero, and dates out of order**

In 2012 a US TV channel showed a tax going from 35 to 39.6 percent. The chart started at 34, not at zero. So the second bar looked five times taller.

Start the chart at zero. The big jump becomes a small step.

The same trick has a twin. In May 2020 the state of Georgia showed daily virus cases. The dates were not in order. So the bars looked like they were going down. Put the dates in order and the drop disappears.

**Better method.** Bars start at zero. Dates go in order. If a small change matters, write the number in words: 4.6 points.

**Ask:** Where does the chart start? Are the dates in order? A chart is a sentence. Read it before you believe it.

**From the news**
- Fox Business, Cavuto, 31 Jul 2012: on air chart "Top tax rate: now 35%, in 2013 39.6%" with the axis starting at 34. Original and corrected version: https://flowingdata.com/2012/08/06/fox-news-continues-charting-excellence/
- Business Insider, May 2020: "A graph shows Georgia bungling its coronavirus data". Posted by the state on 10 May 2020, replaced after a state representative complained. https://www.businessinsider.nl/graph-shows-georgia-bungling-coronavirus-data-2020-5

## Post 5 of 7. Chocolate and Nobel prizes

**Mistake: correlation is not causation**

In 2012 a medical journal showed something funny. Countries that eat more chocolate win more Nobel prizes. The line was almost perfect.

Chocolate does not make people smart. Rich countries buy more chocolate and pay for more universities. Money moves both.

**The same trap today.** Newspapers say a glass of red wine helps you live longer. People who drink a little wine also tend to earn more, exercise more and see a doctor more often. The wine gets the credit.

**Better method.** Look for a third thing that moves both. The best test is a trial where chance decides who gets the treatment. If that is not possible, put the third thing in the model and see if the link disappears.

**Ask:** What third thing moves both? Two lines going up together is not a reason.

**From the news**
- Reuters, 13 Oct 2012: "Eat chocolate, win the Nobel Prize?". The study was in the New England Journal of Medicine, 10 Oct 2012, 23 countries, correlation 0.79. https://www.ft.lk/Lifestyle/eat-chocolate-win-the-nobel-prize/8-114547
- Daily Mail, 30 Apr 2009: half a glass of wine a day can add almost five years to your life. The study followed 1,400 Dutch men for 40 years. Nobody was assigned wine by chance. https://www.decanter.com/wine-news/premature-death-beneficial-61962/

## Post 6 of 7. The test that is 99 percent accurate

**Mistake: base rate neglect**

A disease affects 1 person in 1000. The test is right 99 times out of 100. You test positive. Most people say: you are 99 percent sick. Many doctors say it too.

Look at 1000 people. One is sick. About 10 healthy people also get a positive result. So 11 people are positive and only one is really sick. Your real chance is about 9 percent.

**The same trap in Cardiff, 2017.** Police used face recognition on the crowd at the Champions League final. The system marked 2,470 people as wanted. 2,297 were innocent, because almost nobody in a stadium is on the list.

**Better method.** Do not ask for accuracy. Ask for two numbers. Recall: of the sick people, how many does the test find? Precision: of the positive results, how many are real? Here recall is 100 percent and precision is 9 percent. A confusion matrix shows all four counts in one small table.

**Ask:** How rare was it before the test? A rare disease makes a good test noisy.

**From the news**
- BBC News, 4 May 2018: "2,000 wrongly matched with possible criminals at Champions League". 2,470 alerts, 2,297 wrong, 173 real. Precision 7 percent. https://www.bbc.com/news/uk-wales-south-west-wales-44007872

## Post 7 of 7. The studies in the drawer

**Mistake: publication bias**

74 trials of antidepressant drugs were registered in the US. About half showed the drug did not beat a sugar pill.

Almost all the negative trials were never published, or were written to sound positive. A doctor reading the journals saw 94 percent success. The truth was 51.

**The same trap with Tamiflu.** Governments spent billions on the flu drug. Researchers asked for the full trial data for four years. When they got it, the real benefit was much smaller than the published papers said.

**Better method.** Register every trial before it starts. Then count all of them at the end, published or not. A funnel plot shows the gap where the missing studies should be.

**Ask:** What did not get published? Silence is data too.

**From the news**
- New York Times, 17 Jan 2008: "Antidepressant Studies Unpublished". The study: Turner et al, New England Journal of Medicine, 17 Jan 2008. 37 of 38 positive trials published. Of 36 negative ones, 22 never appeared and 11 were rewritten to sound positive. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18199864/
- The Guardian, 10 Apr 2014: "What the Tamiflu saga tells us about drug trials and big pharma". BMJ report on the four and a half year fight for the data: https://www.bmj.com/content/348/bmj.g2675
